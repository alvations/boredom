\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup, and an honest account of scale}

Our experimental environment cannot reach \texttt{huggingface.co}: the network
policy denies the connection, as it does \texttt{download.pytorch.org}. Public
checkpoints were therefore unavailable and every measurement below is on a model
we trained ourselves. That model satisfies Definition~\ref{def:model} exactly ---
pre-norm residual blocks, RMSNorm, an unembedding --- with $L=6$, $d=128$,
$V=2048$, $1.71$M parameters, trained on a $53$k-token corpus with a held-out
split and early stopping.

We state the consequences plainly rather than in a footnote. The model is
\emph{weak}: validation perplexity is $431$ against a vocabulary of $2048$, i.e.\
about $4.8\times$ better than uniform. Two specific distortions follow. First,
diffuse output distributions overlap more, so measured acceptance rates
$\alpha_\ell = 1 - \TV(p_\ell,p_L)$ are \emph{inflated} relative to a
well-trained model. Second, at $L=6$ the shallowest available exit is
$\rho = 0.167$, whereas a $28$-layer model offers far finer granularity exactly
in the regime where early exit is attractive. Quantitative claims below should
therefore be read as indicative; the qualitative ones
(Sections~\ref{sec:exp-exact} and \ref{sec:exp-cost}) do not depend on scale, and
the two errors we report finding are architecture-independent.

\subsection{Exactness is confirmed at the sampling-noise floor}
\label{sec:exp-exact}

Theorem~\ref{thm:exact} predicts that speculative sampling with \emph{any} draft
returns the target distribution exactly. Testing this against zero would be
meaningless, since any finite sample has positive total variation from $p_L$; the
correct reference is the noise floor, namely $\TV$ between $p_L$ and an equal
number of direct draws from it. Table~\ref{tab:exact} reports both for
$4\times10^{5}$ samples.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrr}
\toprule
layer & $\TV(\text{spec},p_L)$ & $\TV(\text{direct},p_L)$ & ratio
      & $1-\TV(p_\ell,p_L)$ & observed accept \\
\midrule
1 & 0.0195 & 0.0198 & 0.984 & 0.4635 & 0.4634 \\
3 & 0.0202 & 0.0196 & 1.033 & 0.5831 & 0.5833 \\
5 & 0.0186 & 0.0193 & 0.965 & 0.7988 & 0.7978 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Speculative sampling is indistinguishable from direct sampling
(ratio $\approx 1$), and the observed acceptance rate matches the predicted
$1-\TV(p_\ell,p_L)$ to within $10^{-3}$. These rates are measured at a single
position and so differ from the corpus-averaged $\alpha_\ell$ quoted elsewhere;
at $\ell=1$ the single-position value is $0.4635$ against a corpus average of
$0.3274$.}
\label{tab:exact}
\end{table}

This is the result that licenses Corollary~\ref{cor:shortlist}, since the
shortlisted draft assigns zero probability to most of the vocabulary and would be
invalid under any support hypothesis.

\subsection{The acceptance bound is directionally right and numerically useless}

Acceptance rises monotonically with depth exactly as tail energy falls:
$\alpha_\ell = 0.327, 0.383, 0.424, 0.520, 0.693, 1.000$ against
$T_\ell = 20.2, 17.8, 15.4, 12.6, 8.1, 0$. The shape predicted by
Theorem~\ref{thm:accept} is therefore present.

The bound itself is not usable. At $\ell=5$, where $\alpha_5 = 0.693$, feeding
the bound the \emph{measured} logit spread gives $e^{-4.21} = 1.5\times10^{-2}$,
already a factor of $46$ too small; the Cauchy--Schwarz step then inflates the
spread by a further $2.5\times$, landing at $\approx 10^{-5}$.
Remark~\ref{rem:loose} anticipated looseness and attributed it primarily to the
$D_U$ step. That attribution was wrong: the softmax stage alone is fatal, and
Cauchy--Schwarz merely compounds it. A useful bound needs a different proof
strategy, not a tighter constant.

\subsection{A cost-model error, caught by a sanity check}
\label{sec:exp-cost}

The most consequential empirical finding is an error in our own accounting. An
earlier version of Proposition~\ref{prop:cost} charged drafting and verification
but omitted the full-depth pass at the bonus position $t+\gamma$. Evaluated at
$\rho = 1$ it returned $S = 1.735$, which is impossible: at $\rho=1$ the draft
\emph{is} the target, so the procedure degenerates to ordinary autoregressive
decoding and $S = 1$ is forced. Restoring the omitted pass gives exactly
$S = 1.000$ (Remark~\ref{rem:sanity}). We recommend the $\rho\to1$ limit as a
standing check on any speculative-decoding cost model; it is cheap and it caught
an error that survived both derivation and adversarial review.

The same experiment overturned our claim about cache reuse. Measured at $w=1$,
reuse is \emph{worse} at every depth ($2.25$ against $1.50$ at $\rho=0.25$,
$\gamma=2$), because splitting verification across two depths pays for the lower
blocks' weight streaming twice; at $w=0$ it helps, $0.929$ against $0.667$. This
is Proposition~\ref{prop:reuse}, and Remark~\ref{rem:kvreuse} retracts the
earlier claim.

\subsection{The head cost decides whether self-drafting pays}

With the corrected model, the best attainable memory-bound speedup on our model
is $S = 1.026$, at $\ell=1$, $\gamma=1$, with head fraction $u = 0.153$. Setting
$u = 0.26$, the value for Qwen3-0.6B, \emph{no} early-exit layer exceeds $S=1$:
the optimum retreats to the degenerate $\rho=1$. The margin is entirely
determined by the unembedding cost, exactly as Corollary~\ref{cor:ceiling}
predicts, and a $2.6\%$ gain on a model whose acceptance rates are already
inflated is not a result we would build on.

One further asymmetry deserves recording. At $\ell=1$ the sampling-mode
acceptance rate is $\alpha_1 = 0.327$ while top-$1$ agreement between $p_1$ and
$p_L$ is only $0.052$. These govern different deployment modes --- the former
speculative sampling at temperature $1$, the latter greedy decoding --- and the
gap of more than $6\times$ means an acceptance rate that looks tolerable for
sampled decoding is close to useless for greedy decoding. Reported acceptance
rates should always name the mode.

\subsection{Early-exit training rescues thesis 1, marginally}
\label{sec:exp-eetrain}

Remark~\ref{rem:offdist} located the obstruction to self-drafting in the
training objective: intermediate layers are never asked to be decodable. We
tested that claim directly by fine-tuning the same model for $600$ steps with
an auxiliary early-exit loss, $\mathcal{L} = \mathrm{CE}_L + \tfrac12
\mathrm{mean}_{\ell<L}\mathrm{CE}_\ell$ through the shared head, and
re-measuring everything in Sections~\ref{sec:exp-exact}--\ref{sec:exp-cost}.
Table~\ref{tab:eetrain} gives the per-layer effect.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{crrrrrr}
\toprule
$\ell$ & $\alpha_\ell$ before & after & top-1 before & after & $T_\ell$ before & after \\
\midrule
1 & 0.327 & 0.483 & 0.052 & 0.312 & 20.2 & 15.3 \\
2 & 0.383 & 0.586 & 0.115 & 0.354 & 17.8 & 13.0 \\
3 & 0.424 & 0.677 & 0.167 & 0.521 & 15.4 & 10.8 \\
4 & 0.520 & 0.751 & 0.271 & 0.745 & 12.6 & 8.6 \\
5 & 0.693 & 0.844 & 0.552 & 0.839 & 8.1 & 5.8 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Effect of $600$ steps of auxiliary early-exit loss. Acceptance rises
at every layer, greedy top-$1$ agreement rises far more, and the residual tail
energy falls at every layer --- the auxiliary loss moves intermediate states
toward the final one, which is the mechanism by which Theorem~\ref{thm:accept}
says acceptance is governed. Final-layer validation loss rose by $0.08$.}
\label{tab:eetrain}
\end{table}

Three observations. First, exactness is unaffected (TV ratios $1.015$, $0.930$,
$0.998$), as Theorem~\ref{thm:exact} requires of any draft. Second, the
sampling-mode/greedy-mode gap of Section~\ref{sec:exp-cost} largely closes:
at $\ell=1$ top-$1$ agreement rises sixfold while $\alpha_1$ rises by half.
Third, and decisively for the thesis, the corrected memory-bound speedup
becomes
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
head fraction & untuned & early-exit trained \\
\midrule
$u = 0.153$ (this model) & 1.026 & 1.146 \\
$u = 0.260$ (Qwen3-0.6B) & 1.000 & 1.072 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
At Qwen's head fraction the optimum was previously the degenerate $\rho=1$; it
is now a genuine early exit at $\ell=1$. Thesis 1 therefore survives as an
engineering claim on this model --- by $7\%$, after training, at a cost of
$0.08$ in final-layer loss, on a model whose acceptance rates are inflated by
its weakness. We would not build on a margin of that size, but the sign is
the one Remark~\ref{rem:offdist} predicted, and the mechanism (falling
$T_\ell$) is the one the theory names.

The within-round yield decay of the next section is \emph{stronger} after
training: measured $\E[Y] = 1.435$ against a geometric $1.882$ at
$\bar\alpha = 0.483$, with $\Prob[A_1] = 0.340$ already below $\bar\alpha$.
Acceptance measured on held-out text overstates acceptance during decoding,
because decoding visits the model's own continuations; the gap widens as the
draft improves on the text distribution.

\subsection{Yield: acceptance decays faster than geometric}

Running $200$ real speculative rounds at $\ell=1$, $\gamma=4$ gives measured
$\E[Y] = 1.320$ against $1.481$ from the geometric formula of
Corollary~\ref{cor:geom} evaluated at $\bar\alpha = 0.327$. The measured prefix
probabilities are $\Prob[A_j] = 0.250, 0.055, 0.010, 0.005$ against
$\bar\alpha^{j} = 0.327, 0.107, 0.035, 0.011$ under independence: acceptance
decays \emph{faster} than geometric within a round.

This contradicts the remark following Corollary~\ref{cor:geom}, which argued from
Jensen's inequality that cross-context heterogeneity makes the geometric formula
a conservative under-estimate. That argument concerns variation \emph{between}
contexts; within a round the dominant effect runs the other way, because each
accepted draft moves the model onto its own continuation, where the shallow-layer
draft agrees less well. Practitioners estimating speedup from a mean acceptance
rate will therefore \emph{over}-state yield, by $12\%$ here.

We note that the printed identity $\E[Y] = 1 + \sum_j \Prob[A_j]$ is a
bookkeeping check only: both sides are computed from the same run, so it verifies
our accounting rather than testing Proposition~\ref{prop:yield}, which is in any
case a definitional identity.

\subsection{The HMM identity and the impossibility of the bridge}

Theorem~\ref{thm:hmm} is verified against brute-force enumeration over all $M^T$
hidden paths, per $(t,j)$ rather than on aggregates of the final state, for $20$
random HMMs. A negative control confirms the hypothesis split: with a signed
transition matrix the sum-product identity still holds exactly (it is pure
algebra) while the max-product reading breaks, since $(\max,\times)$ distributes
only over $\R_{\ge0}$.

Theorem~\ref{thm:noembed} is confirmed directly. Under a gated linear-attention
update the set of rows on which two trajectories differ stays at $\{1\}$; under
the forward algorithm a single differing coordinate spreads to all of $[m]$ in
one step. The commutator of two observation-conditioned transition operators is
$\|\Psi_0\Psi_1 - \Psi_1\Psi_0\|_{\max} = 4.5\times10^{-2}$, so they are not
simultaneously diagonalisable. Both obstructions hold numerically.

\subsection{Interface bandwidth, and an artefact that would have flattered us}
\label{sec:exp-bandwidth}

Theorem~\ref{thm:stream} predicts that a streaming step machine solves
$\mathrm{PROD}_{N,T}$ iff its interface carries at least $\log_2(N!)$ bits. For
$N=5$ that threshold is $6.91$ bits. Table~\ref{tab:bandwidth} sweeps the
interface width at $T=4$.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrrrr}
\toprule
interface (bits) & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8 & 15 & $\R^{96}$ \\
\midrule
accuracy & 0.359 & 0.545 & 0.614 & 0.786 & 0.852 & 0.917 & 1.000 & 1.000 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Accuracy against interface width, $N=5$, $T=4$. The information-theoretic
threshold is $\log_2 5! = 6.91$ bits. No width below it reaches success, accuracy
is monotone in width, and the success threshold is crossed at $8$ bits.}
\label{tab:bandwidth}
\end{table}

The bound is respected --- no sub-threshold width succeeds --- and degradation
below it is smooth rather than cliff-like, again matching the Fano reading.
Crossing at $8$ rather than $7$ bits indicates roughly one bit of coding
overhead: gradient descent does not find the dense Lehmer code that saturates the
entropy bound exactly. Consistently, an interface of $15$ bits --- the ``natural''
code of five positions at three bits each --- succeeds outright.

We report a negative methodological result alongside it, because it is the one
artefact in this paper that would have \emph{supported} our thesis. Our first
sweep used $T=12$ and found the discrete interface scoring below $0.06$ at every
width from $3$ to $10$ bits while the continuous interface scored $1.000$.
Read naively that is a textbook confirmation: discrete channel bad, continuous
channel good, exactly as the informal bandwidth argument predicts. It is not a
bandwidth effect at all. A bound binding at $6.91$ bits cannot explain failure at
$10$ bits, and the diagnostic is decisive: at $T=12$ even a $20$-bit interface
scores $0.037$, while at $T=4$ a $7$-bit interface scores $0.872$. The binding
constraint at $T=12$ is credit assignment through twelve stacked hard
quantisations, whose straight-through gradients degrade with the number of
bottlenecks on the path. The two failure modes are separable because
optimisation failure is monotone in $T$ while bandwidth failure is monotone in
$b$.

This is the third occasion in this work on which an optimisation artefact
imitated a capacity result, after the undertrained sweep and the one-hot
parameterisation of Section~\ref{sec:exp-capacity}. It is the only one whose
naive reading favoured our own hypothesis, which is precisely why we pursued it.
We suggest the general check: before reading a discrete-bottleneck failure as
evidence of a capacity bound, verify that the failure is monotone in the
bottleneck width and absent at widths well above the claimed threshold.

\subsection{Capacity: the bound is never violated}
\label{sec:exp-capacity}

Theorem~\ref{thm:recall} predicts that a $b$-bit interface retains $n$ binary
values iff $b \ge n$. Table~\ref{tab:capacity} sweeps both.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{crrrr}
\toprule
$b \backslash n$ & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\
\midrule
1 & 0.51 & 0.67 & 0.63 & 0.60 \\
2 & \textbf{1.00} & 0.83 & 0.75 & 0.71 \\
3 & 1.00 & \textbf{1.00} & 0.76 & 0.71 \\
4 & 1.00 & 1.00 & \textbf{1.00} & 0.81 \\
5 & 1.00 & 1.00 & 0.87 & \textbf{1.00} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Recall accuracy by interface width $b$ and number of retained values
$n$. The diagonal $b=n$ is bold. No cell with $b<n$ reaches success, which is
the only direction in which a lower bound can fail.}
\label{tab:capacity}
\end{table}

Nineteen of twenty cells match the prediction. The exception, $b=5$, $n=4$ at
$0.87$, is optimiser noise rather than a capacity effect: $b=4$, $n=4$ reaches
$1.00$ in the same sweep and five bits strictly contain four. Degradation below
the diagonal is graceful ($0.83, 0.76, 0.71$ at $b=n-1$), matching the Fano form
of Theorem~\ref{thm:recall} and contradicting the sharp cliff we originally
pre-registered; that prediction is withdrawn.

Two optimisation artefacts had to be cleared first, and both initially looked
like capacity results. An early sweep at $800$ training steps produced seven
failures \emph{with sufficient capacity}, including $b=4$, $n=2$, where $16$
symbols must hold $2$ bits; no capacity argument can explain that, since $b=3$,
$n=2$ succeeded in the same sweep, and at $3000$ steps the cell reaches $1.000$
on every seed. Separately, parameterising the interface as one symbol from a
codebook of size $2^b$ --- the same $b$ bits --- never cleared threshold at
$n \ge 3$ even at $7000$ steps with two seeds, because it requires the optimiser
to \emph{discover} an injective code through a biased straight-through gradient.
Writing the interface as $b$ independent binary units, so that the natural
solution ``unit $i$ holds value $i$'' is directly representable, produces the
table above. Capacity is identical in both cases; only trainability differs.
